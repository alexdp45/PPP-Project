# Crowded Out: Institutional Capital and the Shift to Off-Market Deals in NJ Industrial Outdoor Storage

*People, Power, and Pay — course project. Working draft; this README will change as the project develops.*

## Motivating fact

Industrial outdoor storage (IOS) — truck yards, equipment storage, and contractor yards — was a niche, fragmented asset class owned mostly by small local operators. Starting around COVID (2020–2021), large amounts of institutional capital entered the sector. From my work in NJ IOS acquisitions, it appears that institutions now win most broker-marketed deals, while smaller operators increasingly buy directly from owners, off-market. ## Question

Has institutional capital pushed smaller operators out of the listed (broker-marketed) market for NJ industrial outdoor storage and into off-market deals? And does the off-market channel behave like a less efficient market, where search effort substitutes for cheap capital?

## Why it matters

- **Owners**, often retiring or long-time local holders, may sell for different prices depending on which channel they use.
- **Smaller operators** have to compete for returns without access to cheap capital.
- **Market structure:** the sector may be consolidating, with small operators acting as intermediaries that buy individual sites off-market and later sell assembled portfolios to institutions.
- **The economic puzzle:** if off-market deals earn better returns, why isn't that advantage competed away?

## Economic connection

The proposed mechanism:

1. **Listed deals work like auctions.** In a broker-run process, the highest bid wins. That usually favors the buyer with the lowest cost of capital.
2. **Cost of capital follows wealth and scale.** In the credit model (Ch. 13), wealthier borrowers get cheaper credit and better terms. Institutions are the "wealthy borrowers" in this market; small sponsors are credit-constrained.
3. **Small operators substitute search for capital.** They spend on outreach, relationships, and handling complex sites to reach sellers who never enter an auction.
4. **Off-market returns may be partly compensation for search costs**, not pure rents. That would explain why they persist: institutions avoid search costs that don't scale.
5. **Incentives inside institutions** (principal–agent): managers paid on capital deployed rather than on performance may bid more aggressively in listed deals.

Other relevant course concepts: rents and competition, search frictions, bargaining power and outside options, and asymmetric information between owners and buyers.

## Testable predictions

1. After 2020, the institutional share of *listed* IOS sales rose while small buyers' share fell.
2. A growing share of small buyers' purchases are off-market.
3. For comparable sites, off-market sale prices are more dispersed than listed prices (a measure of market efficiency).
4. Small operators resell sites to institutions at a premium (repeat sales).
5. Institutions concentrate on large, clean, standardized sites; small buyers on small or complex ones (environmental and zoning issues).

## Known challenges

- **COVID brought more than institutional capital.** E-commerce and logistics demand surged at the same time, and interest rates rose sharply in 2022. Rising prices alone can't show crowding out; the evidence has to come from changes in *who buys through which channel*.
- **Selection:** off-market and listed properties may differ in ways the data doesn't capture.
- **Classifying buyers** from LLC names takes judgment and should be documented.
